Why Parents Are the Key to Education Success

Ms. Aditi Misra has run schools for nearly four decades and raised children through the same years. Her argument is that the partnership between a school and a parent is not a nice-to-have to be switched on in a crisis — it is the thing the child stands on. And almost every conflict inside it can be resolved by conversation.

The short version

Ms. Misra speaks as a principal and as a parent, and she keeps collapsing the distance between the two. Schools that treat parents as adversaries lose the child; schools that treat them as partners get a child who feels safe because both adults are visibly on the same side.

The same principle runs through the harder subjects. Academic pressure eases when a child knows love is not proportional to marks. Career confusion eases when a child gets a reality check instead of an instruction. Device dependence eases when there is something better on offer at home — and when the parent is not on a phone at dinner either. The traits that matter most, she argues, are never taught. They are imbibed, by watching.

In her words

Twelve things worth taking away

Each one below is illustrated with the corresponding page from The Partnership Playbook, the framework built from this conversation.

  1. Parents are partners, not adversaries.

    On her first day as a principal, Ms. Misra was responsible for 900 children and watched dispersal descend into chaos. Two parents stepped in and calmed the rest. The lesson stuck: when parents are on the school’s side, everything downstream runs more smoothly.

  2. Conflict is resolved by conversation, at every level.

    During a dispute over a fee increase, the school did not go quiet. It opened the auditorium and walked parents through the expenses line by line. One parent spoke up for the school’s need to retain good teachers, and the tension broke. Her estimate: transparent conversation resolves nearly every conflict.

  3. Success is not “genetically inspired”.

    Children of high-achieving parents do not automatically become toppers, and children from the most basic backgrounds routinely excel. Treating achievement as inherited quietly damages both groups.

  4. Love cannot be proportional to marks.

    A child’s confidence rests on knowing the affection at home does not rise and fall with a report card. Equating happiness with high marks, a bigger house or a bigger car is the error; the metric that matters is the happiness quotient.

  5. Help a child find a niche — don’t assign one.

    Rather than pushing a child toward medicine, music or engineering, run a reality check: let them shadow a working professional, or perform in public, and find out whether they actually enjoy the daily texture of that life.

  6. The important traits are imbibed, not taught.

    Children learn grit by watching a parent work hard and care for the family. They learn honesty the same way — which is why the small white lie, the “tell them I’m not home”, does more damage than it appears to.

  7. Under 14 or 15, there is little reason for a personal device.

    Beyond supervised research, Ms. Misra sees no case for it. Heavy use, she warns, produces what she calls digital dementia in children — memory loss, sleep loss and forgetfulness, appearing in children as young as 11.

  8. If they can’t go there physically, they can’t go there digitally.

    Her test for cyber safety is that simple. Digital bullying and the dark web are real, and the same permission a parent would require for a physical destination should apply online. Note that half this grid is addressed to the adult.

  9. Rebuild the village deliberately.

    The joint family taught empathy by accident: children watched adults care for sick elders and absorbed it. Nuclear, single-child homes removed that classroom, and violent games moved into the gap. Her answer is to reconstruct the village on purpose — treat the condo or the neighbourhood as extended family, and let the child watch you care for someone.

  10. Fix the dinner table before fixing the child.

    The most operational idea in the episode. Devices leave the room; the question is specific rather than generic; the bad part of the day gets discussed as openly as the good; and the parent answers the same question honestly. That last step is what converts a routine into trust.

  11. The future skills are human ones.

    Dealing with change, collaboration, empathy, compassion and critical thinking. She singles out empathy as the counterweight to the casual, heartless treatment of death in violent video games, and notes that single children in particular have to be taught to share.

  12. Accept the diversity in your own child.

    Imposing a specific will — about IIT, about medical school, about marriage — alienates a child and can open a rift that does not close. When in doubt, she suggests, look back at how your own parents raised you, given you turned out well.

If you only fix one thing

Every practice above sits on a single foundation. Her argument is that you do not have to fight each modern symptom — the lying, the device dependence, the burnout — one at a time. Secure the core and most of them lose their grip.

Ms. Aditi Misra

School Director, DPS International, Gurgaon · Founder Principal, DPS Gurgaon

An alumna of DPS RK Puram, Ms. Misra graduated from Lady Shri Ram College and took her master’s from Delhi University in History and Education. She began teaching at DPS RK Puram in 1985 and has held leadership posts across several Delhi Public Schools — RK Puram, Vasant Kunj and Dubai — before founding and heading the Gurgaon schools. She is also a graduate of the Principals’ Training Centre, Miami.

Questions this episode answers

Why does Aditi Misra call the school-parent partnership non-optional?

Because the child is the beneficiary of it and the casualty without it. She says she has never seen parents as adversaries in the school - they are her partners. When teachers and parents work in sync, a child feels secure knowing both adults genuinely care for them, even when the guidance feels strict. It is also practical: on her first day as a principal, with 900 children and a chaotic dispersal, it was a parent standing up to support the teachers that calmed the crowd and stabilised the environment.

How should a school handle conflict with parents, such as a fee hike?

She describes a four-step sequence. First the trigger, a conflict such as a fee hike, an academic struggle or a disciplinary action. Second the pause: avoid knee-jerk opposition and schedule a face-to-face sit-down. Third the shared reality, where both parties present their view without judgment. Fourth the alignment, which she estimates resolves roughly 99.9 percent of conflicts once both sides realise their ultimate goal is identical - the child's welfare. Her own school used exactly this on a fee dispute, opening the auditorium and explaining the expenses transparently.

Is academic success inherited from high-achieving parents?

No. She is direct that success is not purely genetically inspired: children of high-achieving parents do not always become toppers, and children from basic backgrounds frequently excel. Treating results as hereditary sets up unfair expectations in both directions.

How should parents handle exam stress and academic pressure?

By separating love from marks. In the conditional pattern, love is proportional to results - 100 means praise and 40 means withdrawal - which produces pushback, anxiety and a child who hides failure. In the unconditional pattern, a 40 is a signal to evaluate what distracted the child rather than a reason to reduce affection, which produces confidence, open communication and self-correction. She also warns against equating happiness with high marks, a large house or a big car.

How can parents help a child choose a career without forcing one?

Let the real world deliver the feedback instead of the parent. If a child declares an unrealistic aspiration, the dictator path - 'no, study engineering' - produces resentment and a child who assumes they were a misunderstood genius. The facilitator path arranges a real-world test, such as singing where strangers can respond honestly. The child then self-corrects based on reality rather than parental decree, and the relationship stays intact. Educate yourself on their dream first, and do not be the bad guy.

What is digital dementia in children?

It is her term for what she observes in heavy device users: memory loss, severe sleep disruption, forgetfulness, loss of empathy, and even an inability to blink normally in 11 and 12-year-olds. Her key insight is about fairness - asking a child to detox after giving them unlimited access is unfair. You cannot intoxicate them and then demand a detox. The solution is preventing the dependency in the first place.

At what age should a child get a personal device?

Her position is that children under about 14 have no need for personal device access. Academic research should be supervised in a common area of the home, which prevents both inappropriate exposure and digital bullying. She pairs this with physical counterweights: move children from school directly into physical hobbies, sport or extended family time, because children engaged in physical arts rarely develop device addiction.

What is her rule for cyber safety and children's digital privacy?

A child cannot wander the physical world without permission, so they must not wander the dark web without oversight - protect them digitally as you do physically. She is explicit that minors do not get digital privacy. Half of her guardrails are aimed at parents rather than children: phones are not extensions of the hand, and should not be placed on restaurant tables or used as pacifiers for children in public.

What is the Dinner Table Diagnostic?

A four-step routine for daily family conversation. One, device quarantine: all devices are physically removed from the dining area. Two, the memory prompt: ask what was the best part of your day and what was the worst part, rather than the generic 'how was your day' which yields a one-word answer. Three, normalising the negative: by discussing the worst part, children learn not to bottle up failure. Four, mutual vulnerability: parents answer the prompt too, sharing a bad day at the office or a financial constraint, which makes the child a partner in reality and builds trust.

Why does she say character is imbibed rather than taught?

Because children absorb the nuances of what adults actually do. A mother who goes to work despite a mild fever so her students are cared for teaches that work is worship, and the child develops lifelong grit without ever being lectured. A mother who tells a child to say she is not at home teaches that lying is acceptable, and the child begins habitually lying at school. You cannot act one way and expect different behaviour from your child.

How can parents counteract the empathy deficit?

She traces the loss to the disappearance of the multi-generational joint family, where caring for sick elders was naturally observed and imbibed, replaced by isolated single-child nuclear homes. Meanwhile empathy is being overwritten by violent games in which consequences are reduced to a red splash on a screen. Her solution is to artificially reconstruct the village: treat your apartment complex or neighbourhood society as extended family, and actively demonstrate neighbourly care so the child has a model for compassion.

Which skills does she consider essential for the future?

Four. Adaptability to change, because the world now changes yearly rather than by the decade - she suggests not fighting school section changes and letting children learn to make new friends. Collaboration, because the future does not reward isolated geniuses and single children in particular must learn to share. Creative and critical thinking, the ability to navigate out of a brain freeze when the parameters suddenly change. And deep empathy, which she calls the ultimate differentiator in a tech-saturated world.

What is the single thing parents should get right?

Anchor the child's baseline in unconditional love and open conversation. She argues that when that core is secure, the modern symptoms - lying, tech addiction, academic burnout - are naturally mitigated. You do not need to fight every symptom if the ecosystem is healthy.

What is her final message to parents?

Look back when in doubt: you likely turned out fine, so model the best parts of how your own parents raised you and adapt them to the modern context. Accept your child's differences rather than imposing a specific will about IIT, medical school or marriage. Her closing thought is to keep that child closest to your heart, because it would be horrible for parents and children to drift apart over these differences. The ultimate metric is joyful learning - if the child is not experiencing joy, the ecosystem requires adjustment.
