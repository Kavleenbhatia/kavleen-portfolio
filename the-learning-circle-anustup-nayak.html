Why 70% of Children Struggle to Learn — And How Teachers Can Change That

Across India, nearly 70% of children in Grade 3 still struggle to read or do basic math. Anustup Nayak of the Central Square Foundation argues the problem isn’t the children — their working memory simply fills up before basic skills become automatic — and walks through how structured pedagogy is changing that across 11 Indian states.

The short version

Nayak leads classroom instruction practices at Central Square Foundation, which works with 11 state governments on structured pedagogy — a teaching approach built on four pillars: goal setting, pedagogical tools, capacity building, and assessment and data. The premise is that basic reading and math, simple as they look to an adult, take enormous effort for a child still building working memory, and that once those skills become automatic by age eight to ten, everything else in school gets easier to learn.

The conversation walks through what a child’s struggle to read or subtract actually looks like from the inside, why Nayak compares a teacher’s job to an air traffic controller making 2,000 to 3,000 micro-decisions a day, and a real classroom that went from 2% to 60% numeracy proficiency after adding just ten minutes of daily independent practice. It closes on where India’s foundational literacy push stands today, and what school leaders and parents can each do next.

Five things worth taking away

  1. Basic skills eat up a child’s entire working memory.

    Working memory acts like a narrow funnel for new information before it reaches long-term memory. A child who hasn’t mastered decoding spends it all recognizing individual letters, and by the third word of a sentence has already forgotten the first.

  2. Teaching is closer to air traffic control than lecturing.

    A teacher makes 2,000 to 3,000 micro-decisions a day — what to teach, who needs help, how to manage the room, all at once. Nayak’s point: the fix is better systems, not better heroes.

  3. CSF’s structured pedagogy rests on four pillars.

    Goal setting, pedagogical tools, capacity building, and assessment and data — used across 11 state governments to make good teaching a predictable output rather than an accidental anomaly.

  4. India’s progress is real, but the gap is still wide.

    Grade 3 reading proficiency rose from 16.3% to 23.4% between 2022 and 2024, yet 70–75% of children remain below grade level, held back by multi-grade classrooms, language mismatches, and administrative load on teachers.

  5. Ten minutes a day moved one classroom from 2% to 60%.

    In one district, adding just ten minutes of daily independent practice in literacy and numeracy lifted proficiency from 2% to 60% in a matter of months — proof that small, structured inputs compound fast.

Questions this episode answers

Why do so many Indian children struggle with basic reading and math?

It is less about ability and more about working memory. A child who hasn’t automated basic decoding spends all of their limited working memory on recognizing letters or counting, leaving nothing left to actually understand what they read or solve.

What is the working-memory bottleneck, and why does it matter?

Working memory acts like a narrow funnel for new information before it reaches long-term memory. If foundational skills are not automatic, that funnel stays clogged, and children cannot move on to higher-order thinking in math, science or social studies.

What is structured pedagogy?

A defined, teachable method for classroom instruction, built around technique, tools and training, and organized under four pillars: goal setting, pedagogical tools, capacity building, and assessment and data.

Does structured pedagogy leave room for teacher creativity?

Nayak’s framing: good teaching is 20% knowledge and 80% understanding of children. Structure removes the burden of stitching together resources so teachers can spend their energy and creativity on individual students, not on rebuilding lesson plans from scratch.

What is India’s FLN mission, and how is it progressing?

The National Education Policy 2020 and the NIPUN Bharat mission put foundational literacy and numeracy at the center of Indian education policy. Grade 3 reading proficiency rose from 16.3% to 23.4% between 2022 and 2024, real progress, though 70–75% of children are still below grade level.

What can parents do to support foundational learning at home?

Ensure regular school attendance, have daily conversations to build vocabulary, and consider low-cost apps such as Chimple. Nayak is direct that the first three to four years of school are the most critical window.
