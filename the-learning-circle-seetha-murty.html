How One Leader Leads 7,000 Students Across 5 Campuses

Dr. Seetha Murty, Director at Silver Oaks International Schools, Hyderabad, on the Homegrown Leadership Model, why she doesn’t believe in SOPs, and what parents actually notice when a school is working — drawn from 21 years running schools at scale.

The short version

Dr. Murty runs five campuses and 7,000 students on a principle she calls Homegrown Leadership: principals are promoted from within rather than hired in, so the person leading a campus already carries its culture rather than importing a new one.

Her sharpest position is against standard operating procedures as the default management tool. Where SOPs try to script behaviour, she argues for building shared mission and understanding people instead — rules can’t anticipate every classroom, but a teacher who understands the “why” can. As she puts it in the episode, teachers “are not your subordinates, they’re your team.”

The conversation moves from mission over presence, to getting reluctant teachers on board, to what parents actually notice when a school is functioning well — closing with a message to students and the line the episode is titled around: leadership, stripped of hierarchy, is a responsibility you carry alone.

Six things worth taking away

The paradox underneath all six: the bigger the system got, the less her physical presence could hold it together.

  1. Shared mission beats physical presence.

    A leader can’t be on every campus at once. What travels in her absence is a mission the staff have actually internalised, not a set of instructions waiting to be enforced.

  2. Homegrown Leadership: promote from within.

    Principals who rise through the system already understand its culture, its people and its constraints — they don’t need to be onboarded into the mission, because they helped carry it.

  3. Skip rigid SOPs — understand people instead.

    Her stated reason for not leaning on standard operating procedures: they standardise behaviour without building the judgment to handle what the procedure didn’t anticipate.

  4. Every teacher is a leader in their own classroom.

    Treating teachers as a team rather than subordinates changes how much ownership they take of outcomes — the framing she returns to repeatedly through the episode.

  5. The standing test: “Is this right for students?”

    A single question used to cut through competing priorities when a decision gets complicated.

    Two moments from the episode show that test being applied to decisions that look small on paper.

    And a second, where the same question produced a very different answer from the obvious one.

  6. What parents actually notice.

    Not the policies on paper, but signals that are harder to fake — covered in the “What parents notice” chapter of the conversation.

    What follows from that, in her account, is the part schools rarely measure.

Where the thread leads

The conversation ends wider than it starts. These last four panels step back from the individual practices to the system they add up to.

Asked what the next decade asks of schools, Dr. Murty does not answer with structures or technology. Her answer is about the kind of judgment a school builds in its people before the situation arrives.

The compass she describes is deliberately simple. When priorities compete and the decision gets complicated, one question settles it — the same question she applies everywhere else in the system.

Seen together, the pieces stop looking like separate policies. Mission, homegrown leaders, teacher ownership and the habit of reflection each hold the others up.

Which is what the invisible thread actually is: not a chain of command reaching five campuses, but a shared understanding that keeps working when the leader is not in the room.

Questions this episode answers

What is leadership beyond presence?

Building a culture strong enough that leadership keeps working even when the leader isn’t physically there — the core distinction Dr. Murty draws between mission-driven and presence-driven leadership.

How do you lead multiple campuses?

Through shared mission, internal promotion of leaders who already carry that mission, and prioritising the “why” behind a decision over a fixed SOP.

What makes a great school leader?

Reflection, a focus on people over process, and genuinely empowering teachers rather than managing them as subordinates.

What is Homegrown Leadership?

Promoting principals from within the system rather than hiring externally, so the people leading a campus already understand and carry its culture forward.
