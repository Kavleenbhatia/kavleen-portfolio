India’s Schools Have a Mentoring Problem, Not a Training Problem

Dr. Ashok K. Pandey has spent over three decades running schools and shaping teacher policy through CBSE and NCTE. His diagnosis is uncomfortable: India keeps training teachers and keeps producing accidental principals. What’s missing isn’t another workshop — it’s a mentor.

The short version

India does not lack teacher training. It runs pre-service programmes, in-service programmes and a new four-year integrated degree. What it lacks is the thing every other profession takes for granted: a senior person who holds your hand through the first years. Dr. Pandey calls the gap a deficiency of “Vitamin LD” — leadership development.

What is the difference between training a teacher and mentoring one?
Training tells a teacher how to integrate AI. Mentoring shows her how to use AI to build critical thinking in children — then hands her a workshop to run, a paper to present, a responsibility to own. Training transfers information; mentoring transfers trust.
What is the “accidental principal” problem?
In most Indian schools you become a principal by virtue of years served, not by preparation. The result is a leader equipped for administration but never mentored to design learning or shape culture.
What should a school do about “we train teachers and they leave”?
Invert the maths. Cost out an untrained teacher who stays ten years against a trained teacher who leaves after two. A teacher who leaves well-formed becomes your ambassador — and that is marketing budget you no longer spend.
Is AI a threat to teachers?
Dr. Pandey argues AI is not a disruptor but an exposer: it reveals how narrow our definition of learning has been. He asks teachers to treat AI as a colleague and as the fourth teacher, after the classroom teacher, the parent and the peer group.

The conversation connects closely with a question Kavleen explores in her own work: how educators build structured practice in using AI as a thinking partner before it becomes an execution shortcut. Schools that have run her workshops, including DPS Vasant Vihar and Indraprastha International School, Dwarka, report teachers leaving able to use the method immediately, not just aware it exists. More on the framework: IMPACTS.

Nine things worth taking away

  1. AI isn’t the disruption. It’s the diagnostic.

    If a task can be outsourced to a machine, it was never a good measure of learning. The question Dr. Pandey puts to his fraternity: are we measuring judgment, wisdom, creativity and values — or recall?

  2. Redesign pedagogy first, assessment second.

    Most reform starts with the exam. He argues you cannot fix assessment until you have decided what the classroom dialogue is actually for.

  3. Student agency is latent heat.

    A physics student’s metaphor: agency is already inside the child, hidden and unexpressed. The facilitator’s job is to release it, not to install it.

  4. Every classroom is now a flipped classroom.

    Children arrive having already met the information. The teacher’s scarce value has moved from delivering knowledge to curating it — and to teaching children to hold judgment rather than rush it.

  5. AI is the fourth teacher — and your colleague.

    “When you are having a staff meeting with four teachers, keep in mind that your fifth teacher is also sitting there.”

  6. India’s deficiency is Vitamin LD.

    The diagnosis evolved: first Vitamin T (teachers), then Vitamin TL (teacher leadership), now Vitamin LD — leadership development. The cracked-foundation diagram at the top of this page is the whole argument in one image.

  7. Onboarding is not a luxury. It is the sprouting period.

    Two to three months paid simply to observe: corridors, classrooms, playground, library. “It is like putting a seed in for three months — then it will sprout.”

  8. Compliance can become an inner drive — if the culture is built for it.

    Which is why he calls principals not administrators but drivers of learning and culture. The practical test is whether your development programme ever hands someone real responsibility.

  9. Teaching sits in the safe zone of the automation charts.

    He cites the World Economic Forum’s Future of Jobs work: teaching, counselling, advising and mentoring carry low replacement risk. His warning: don’t take that for granted — earn it by preserving the human part of the job.

If you are a teacher who wants to lead

The most practical stretch of the episode. Asked what a teacher should actually do to move toward leadership in three to five years, Dr. Pandey gave five moves — none of which require permission from anyone.

On the first point he is blunt about why it matters: children will not love a mediocre teacher, but they will follow one who is exceptional at a single visible thing. His example is a colleague whose only differentiator was the best handwriting on campus — students queued for his notes. Hear the full roadmap from 28:07.

Questions this episode answers

What does “India has a mentoring problem, not a training problem” mean?

India runs a great deal of teacher training — pre-service, in-service, and now the four-year ITEP degree. What it does not run at scale is mentoring: a senior practitioner who holds a new teacher’s hand with compassion, empathy and non-judgmental intent through the first years, and who keeps doing it as that teacher moves toward leadership. Training transfers content. Mentoring transfers judgment.

What is “Vitamin LD”?

Dr. Pandey’s shorthand for the missing nutrient in Indian schooling. He first wrote that India suffered a deficiency of Vitamin T — teachers. He revised it to Vitamin TL — teacher leadership. He now calls it Vitamin LD: leadership development.

What is the practical difference between professional development and mentoring?

His example: teaching a staff how to integrate AI is professional development. Showing a teacher how to use AI to build critical thinking in children — demonstrating it, then giving her a workshop to run, a paper to present at a conference, a chance to author something, sponsorship for a bigger responsibility — is mentoring. “Start trusting her with responsibilities. That is mentoring.”

What is the “accidental principal” problem?

Where there is no structured leadership development, people become principals by seniority. They are, at best, accidental principals: prepared to run an administration, never mentored to lead a culture, design learning, or build a place where human flourishing happens.

How should a school answer “what if we invest in teachers and they leave?”

Three ways, he says. First, do the other sum: what does it cost to not invest and keep that teacher for ten years? Second, a teacher who leaves well-formed becomes your ambassador wherever she goes — that is marketing you no longer pay for. Third, the children she teaches next are still this country’s children. “It is not a lose game. It is a win-win situation.”

What five things should a teacher do to move into school leadership?

From the roadmap at 28:07 —

  1. Be an excellent teacher, not an ordinary one. Be visibly excellent in at least one thing; children love a teacher who is exceptional at something.
  2. Learn from those worth emulating. In a staffroom of fifty, find the ones you want to become and ask them how they do it.
  3. Raise your hand. Volunteer for the conference nobody wants, the responsibility everyone stepped back from.
  4. Read widely and network across schools. Ten maths teachers from ten schools meeting monthly, even online, is a professional development programme nobody has to fund.
  5. Find a mentor inside your school and one outside it. Only then will you know what mentoring is capable of.
What does “AI as the fourth teacher” mean?

A child already has three teachers: the one in the classroom, the parent at home, and the peer group. AI becomes the fourth — and because it is the classroom teacher’s colleague, it belongs in the staff meeting too. “Your fifth teacher is also sitting there, not visible in the physical sense but visible in every other sense.”

What is ITEP, and why does it matter here?

The Integrated Teacher Education Programme, introduced after NEP 2020: a four-year course a student can choose straight after Class 12, the way one chooses engineering or law. Dr. Pandey welcomes it — but points out where the parallel breaks. An engineer gets two or three years of internship. A new teacher walks into a classroom on day one, having not seen one since her own schooling.

Where can an individual teacher find mentoring without waiting for their school?

He points to the National Mission for Mentoring on the NCTE portal, where teachers can enrol as mentees and attend expert-led sessions several times a week; to SWAYAM, DIKSHA, NISHTHA and open courses from IITs Bombay and Madras; and — only half in jest — to AI itself: “You can share your career aspirations and create a plan together.”

If AI can do it, is it really learning?

00:53

Are we measuring the values and the skills the world needs in order to flourish, and that human beings themselves need in order to flourish? Will that become part of our definition of learning, or will it remain what it is today — what can be measured in terms of what you produce, what you recall, what you write? That is the age-old definition of learning. So when I say that if everything can be outsourced and everything can be produced through AI, then where is the learning in this whole game?

02:12

Kavleen Kaur: You have mentioned a few skills that we should be measuring. Instead of just recall-based questions — if I run a school, what do you suggest we do differently?

02:25

Ashok K. Pandey: We should redesign our assessments altogether to measure what actually drives learning. But redesign the pedagogy first. Redesign the learning first. What is it that we want to teach in the classroom — and “teaching” is perhaps not the right word now. What is it we want to have a dialogue about in our classrooms? It is basically a dialogic process we are talking about. Is it going to continue to be teacher-led classrooms, or does it have to be student-led classrooms, with children making meaning out of what we are doing? Or is it just compliance with a syllabus and covering of a syllabus? Is there any element of real-life connect? Flipped classrooms and student agency as latent heat

03:28

Kavleen Kaur: You said it has to be student-led, because earlier it was teacher-led — mainly a one-way exchange of information. With AI, that information exchange may happen outside the class.

03:44

Ashok K. Pandey: A very nice thing you have pointed out. Some years ago something came into the pedagogical jargon — the flipped classroom. A very simple definition: you announce in advance that this is what we are going to discuss tomorrow or the day after, children have the learning material, they engage with it, they come back to the classroom, and instead of the teacher leading it, the students lead it. The teacher becomes part of the classroom. One child leads the discussion, somebody interjects, somebody gives a new input — the whole scenario has been flipped. That was the idea, and it was touted as one of the innovative pedagogies.

04:33

Ashok K. Pandey: But look at the times that have changed, and at the speed at which the change has come. Every day we are having a flipped class. The children are all the time engaging with new knowledge — if not knowledge, then at least information, data. Teachers are no longer the sole repositories of knowledge. So the information is there. But how do you curate it? How do you become a curator of that information so that you shift from information to knowledge to wisdom — meaning-making, as we call it?

05:05

Ashok K. Pandey: When we talk of a student-led class, what we are essentially saying is: do away with the assumption that children do not know anything. They know, and they know a lot. Give them the opportunity to come out with what they know and what they want to say. And that takes us to another thing we have to explore in our classrooms — student agency. As a student of physics I call agency the latent heat. It is hidden. And we, as facilitators, have to bring that heat, that potential, that capability out. That is what a student-led classroom is, and that will change the whole scenario in the classroom.

Misinformation and the teacher’s evolving role

06:00

Kavleen Kaur: Two points from what you said. One is agency as latent heat. But also — students have a lot of this agency, this heat, this information, and along with it a lot of misinformation today. So isn’t it also essential that while we develop their agency, the teacher’s role is to prevent or reduce that misinformation?

06:25

Ashok K. Pandey: What you have said gives at least one clear reason for teachers to be in the classroom. There is already talk that AI will replace teachers. You have just given one instance; I will give you a few more during our conversation, of why teachers cannot be replaced. This role was always there, but it is more exposed today and more intense today, because the information is coming and the children are not mature enough to apply scrutiny to it — to its authenticity, to its potential to affect your behaviour, your psyche, your emotions. That is where the larger role of teachers comes in.

07:14

Kavleen Kaur: Even the speed at which children consume this information isn’t really appropriate. They jump through whole chapters and think they have understood, but the learning may not be deep enough. So instead of just letting students cover the breadth of learning, the teacher takes them into the depths of it.

07:38

Ashok K. Pandey: Yes — and teachers have to acknowledge that in a classroom context we are in an attention economy. Attention is in crisis and the children are not able to cope with it. That is where the ability of the teacher comes in: to tell them how to apply their judgment, and if you can’t apply your judgment, at least can you hold your judgment? Can you delay the whole process of arriving at a judgment? Even that will be helpful. The moment we jump to judgments, we play into the hands of heightened emotions. If we delay those judgments, we also allow the emotions to come down. And then our judgments will be much better, because by that time more information will have reached us, and that information may regulate the process of judgment.

AI as the fourth teacher

08:50

Ashok K. Pandey: I have been telling teachers for at least two years: treat AI as your colleague. You should not be afraid of a colleague. AI is the fourth teacher for the students. Why? Because they already have their teacher in the classroom. They have parents as teachers at home. And they have the peer group. These three distinct entities work as teachers in their lives, and AI becomes their fourth teacher — because it is the colleague of your teacher. When you are having a staff meeting with four teachers, please keep in mind that your fifth teacher is also sitting there, not visible in the physical sense of the term, but visible in every other sense of the term.

09:30

Ashok K. Pandey: And we still retain the right and the wisdom to harness the best of AI, and we should develop our own best potential. Therefore the ideal situation we are going to deal with as teachers is the best of AI and the best of the teacher.

School leaders are shaping future citizens

09:51

Kavleen Kaur: You have also said that this transformation from information to knowledge to wisdom needs the guidance of very effective, strong school leaders. School leaders are not just managing a school; they are shaping future citizens, and they are building the next generation of school leaders. So have we now moved into the area of teacher leadership development?

10:28

Ashok K. Pandey: That is something very important. First of all, India has been a land of teachers, and teachers have historically been called guru, acharya, upadhyaya — by various names, which means that the manifestation of an individual into a teacher is not one thing but a variety of things a teacher is supposed to do. Unfortunately, today we talk of a teacher’s role only in the terms we have discussed in the last ten or fifteen minutes: you have to teach, you have to prepare them for admissions, for cracking examinations and competitions. That is the overwhelming role we have assumed for ourselves.

11:14

Ashok K. Pandey: But as teachers we do not have to talk only about livelihood. We have to talk about life. We have to talk about the flourishing of the human being. Aristotle put it beautifully — when his students asked him what the purpose of education is, he said the purpose of education is human flourishing. And when they asked him to elaborate, he said human flourishing is a combination of two things: the cognitive flourishing of the human being and the character flourishing of the human being.

11:49

Ashok K. Pandey: But in our modern wisdom, for the last hundred years or so — or a little less, since independence — we have focused only on the cognitive side. And even there we narrowed it down to rote memorisation, to production, to recall, a little bit of understanding, even less application, and certainly not analysis, synthesis, creativity, innovation or problem-solving in a multi-perspective scenario. Those things have not happened. And character building has gone entirely out of the picture.

Pre-service training, ITEP, and the career perception problem

12:45

Ashok K. Pandey: I have given you that background so that I can clearly set the agenda of teacher development for the future. What are the instruments of teacher development in this country? Pre-service, in-service, and the incidental and accidental development of teachers. I will not talk much about pre-service, because it has not covered itself in a lot of glory — though I certainly acknowledge the good efforts that have been made. In-service training is happening a great deal, and I appreciate it, because that is the only way we can allow teachers to flourish. 13:06

Ashok K. Pandey: And for that I don’t blame only the institutions or the system. I also blame the social perception of the role of the teacher, and of the status of the teacher in society today. See the barrier we have created. Overcoming it is so difficult. So the only hope is this: for whatever reason you joined the profession, can we neutralise that negativity? Can we neutralise that deficient motivation and start on a clean slate, where we can make you a good, a better and the best teacher?

14:06

Ashok K. Pandey: I don’t want your viewers to get disappointed here. Just as you choose to become an engineer, a doctor, a lawyer or a fashion designer at the end of Class 12, you now have the opportunity to choose to be a teacher at Class 12. 15:14

Ashok K. Pandey: But the similarity stops there, and the differences emerge. After becoming an engineer you have two or three years of internship, training and exposure. That is where teacher development comes in. We have to tell them what teaching is about, what learning is about. And one thing they have to learn is that in this profession learning never stops. Teaching is a misnomer. It is only learning, learning and learning. 16:10

Ashok K. Pandey: One of the most profound sayings in our wisdom civilisation says exactly this: that the teacher and the student are co-learners. They learn together, they grow together, they shine together, they reap the benefits of learning and knowledge together, and there is no animosity between the two. This is the principle we have to hone in young, aspiring teachers.

India’s Vitamin LD deficiency — from training to mentoring

16:41

Kavleen Kaur: That is very empowering. From what you shared, a couple of things have to happen for this to succeed. One, the teaching profession has to become more sought-after, more respected. And two, teachers don’t get an opportunity to do an internship, so they miss the kind of mentoring other professions have. You have said India doesn’t have a teacher training problem — it has a mentoring problem. What value can mentoring add here, and how do we build systems where that becomes possible?

17:26

Ashok K. Pandey: Let me put it this way. Some time ago I wrote an article saying that India is suffering from a deficiency of Vitamin T — T for teachers. Later I modified it and said India is suffering from a deficiency of Vitamin TL — teacher leadership. And today I say we are suffering from a deficiency of Vitamin LD — leadership development. That is exactly where you are coming from.

18:04

Ashok K. Pandey: Traditionally the basic presumption was that children come to school knowing nothing, and therefore anybody who knows something is capable of telling them everything. That assumption does not stand the test of scrutiny today. We even celebrated it — we said children are a clean slate and you have the liberty to write anything on it. That had its relevance and meaning in one era. But I don’t think children are a clean slate now. Children are a slate full of information and experience. And we have to sift the good from the bad; we have to curate it.

18:53

Ashok K. Pandey: Therefore leadership development is very important. And for that, the seniors in the profession, the policy makers — and not only seniors in teaching, but dedicated people in every other walk of life — we have to expose our young teachers to them. We have to stretch their experience. We have to make them reflective teachers. They have to ask themselves two or three questions every day. What is the purpose of my being in a school? Am I coming up to the expectations of the learners? What changes am I going to make to meet those expectations? Am I justifying my role here? Those are the reflections we have to start with. And we have to become fully effective teachers — our productivity has to be at the optimum level.

Teaching is a mission, not a profession

19:51

Ashok K. Pandey: Teaching is more than a profession. It is a mission. And this mission is that you are designing the future of young children. When you are designing the future of young children, you are also designing the future of communities, of societies, of nations, of humanity. Therefore your responsibility is much greater than that of anyone in any other profession. You can produce the best product in a manufacturing company — but here you are dealing with human beings. I think you need more capability, more agency, more potential and more sensitivity in the teaching profession than anywhere else.

20:40

Ashok K. Pandey: And this lesson will not come from any pre-service training. It will come only when they come to the classroom. When a teacher is appointed, do at least two to three months of onboarding. Let the teacher understand the whole thing. Feel the environment. Feel the children. Go through the corridors, move into any classroom, see what is happening. Go to the playground, go to the library. For three months you are paid just to learn. It is like putting a seed in for three months — then it will sprout, and it will come out. And the teacher says: I am going to do it like this. I will not do it the way I have seen it for three months. Not because those things are being done wrongly — but I will do it differently, because I am more innovative now. I can experiment more. I will try new experiments. I will not go by tradition.

21:37

Ashok K. Pandey: But for that, the seniors have to hold hands — with compassion, with empathy, with a futuristic and non-judgmental intention. In fact this intentionality in leader development is what is missing. In most places it is compliance-driven professional development. Compliance is not what is important. What is important is: are we developing consciousness about something? Are we developing curiosity? Are we developing systems where compliance becomes an inner drive? Let it be compliance in the beginning, but it has to transform into an inner drive. That will come from creating a great culture of learning on the campus. And that is why I call principals, headmasters, anybody heading an institution, the drivers of learning and culture.

Why school leaders resist change — and the ROI reframe

22:30

Kavleen Kaur: Then why is there so much resistance to change? Why can’t we allow teachers three months to settle down, to learn, to be mentored? If I am a school leader, how would you convince me? Because unless things change at the ground level, the outcome will not change.

22:54

Ashok K. Pandey: Let me tell you a couple of things to make this conversation lighter. Some years ago, at a conference, a shocker was thrown at me. A very respected leader got up and said: what is your answer to the question that you invest so much in teachers, and then they leave?

23:24

Ashok K. Pandey: Look where we are coming from. If we are measuring investment in somebody in terms of return on investment, then some mindset change is required. So I have been requesting people: tell me, you spend X on a teacher who is with you — that is the cost of the investment — and let us say that teacher leaves after two years. Simple mathematics. Now, you don’t invest that X, and the teacher remains with you for ten years. What is the cost of that?

24:00

Ashok K. Pandey: That is one thing. The second: even if that teacher leaves, with all those values and skills and wisdom and knowledge, wherever she goes she will be your ambassador. She will always attribute whatever she is to you and your school. So you will be saving your marketing budget — calculate that saving too. And the third thing: if she is imparting that good practice to some other group of children, they are also children of this country, of this world. So it is not a lose game. We are not losing. It is a win-win situation.

24:42

Ashok K. Pandey: I am not saying everybody is grappling only with this dilemma. There are many other issues. Our whole system is such — the pressure of time, the timetabling, syllabus completion and many other things. We are not teaching, actually; there is a lot of administration in it. Those are also reasons, so we have to be a little more compassionate here. There are many things AI can help us automate. We can reduce the workload, reduce the mundane things teachers do, and free up time in a teacher’s routine — for self-growth, for studying, for learning, for being with the children, which is the primary job of a teacher.

25:36

Ashok K. Pandey: Having said that, I want to reiterate: comparing what I saw twenty years ago with what I see today, there is a sea change in the attitude of managements and principals — yes, professional development is very important. Do it in the name of mentoring. Because professional development will still be very compliant, very information-driven or skill-driven, whereas mentoring will always be human-driven. Their capability as human beings, their capability to apply wisdom, to work in teams, to take decisions, to appreciate the higher purpose of education, to develop relationships — those are the things we have to develop.

Professional development vs mentoring — the practical difference

26:20

Kavleen Kaur: Help us see, in practical terms, what the difference is between training a teacher and mentoring a teacher — both if I am a teacher, and if I am in school leadership.

26:35

Ashok K. Pandey: Thank you for highlighting this. Take an example. How to use AI could be part of professional development. How to integrate AI — that is professional development. But how to use AI to develop critical thinking in children is a little different. Somebody has to apply it, show it, demonstrate it, and only then will a new teacher appreciate it.

27:06

Ashok K. Pandey: And then, you have given the professional development to the teacher — fine. Now give her the opportunity to conduct a workshop. Give her the opportunity to present a paper at a conference. Give her the opportunity to be an author. Sponsor her for greater responsibilities. Start trusting her with responsibilities. That is mentoring.

The accidental principal problem

27:32

Ashok K. Pandey: As of today there is no mentoring, no structured development of leadership. You become a principal by virtue of the number of years you have served. At best you become an accidental principal, without having been mentored to be a principal. You become a principal to show your acumen for administration — but not for leading a culture, not for designing learning, and not for designing spaces where human flourishing takes place. That is what has to change.

Five-step roadmap for aspiring school leaders

28:07

Kavleen Kaur: If I am a teacher who aspires to move into a leadership role three or five years from now, and I have good leadership above me — what is the path? What are the activities, the initiatives I should take?

28:22

Ashok K. Pandey: Five or six things are important. Number one: be an excellent teacher. Don’t be an ordinary teacher. Be a rockstar teacher. And to be a rockstar teacher you have to go beyond your textbook. You have to go beyond the routine of going into the classroom and coming out of the classroom. You have to invest your time and energy in your own growth. You have to be an excellent teacher, because children today do not expect you to be mediocre or ordinary. They will not love you.

29:03

Ashok K. Pandey: In my experience, children love a teacher who is excellent in at least one thing. I had a colleague who was extremely popular — children would die for him. Do you know what he excelled at? The best handwriting on the whole campus. He would make notes the children loved; it looked as if it were printed. That was the differentiator. I’m giving a common example. Equally, a teacher who sings very nicely, or excels at a sport, or is very good at communication, or is always available to the children — a teacher who can take a child aside and ask, tell me, what is wrong with you today, you are not looking as happy as you did yesterday. These are the differentiators. We have to develop these qualities.

30:08

Ashok K. Pandey: The second thing: a young teacher joins an organisation where there are fifty other teachers, of at least five or ten varieties, if not fifty. Learn from those who are worth emulating. Learn from them. Emulate them. Walk up to them and ask: how do you manage it, how do you do it, what do you advise me? Then you become a person who does not shy away from taking responsibility, who does not shy away from coming to the front when many others have moved back. If you do this in your initial years, apart from the other benefits, it also becomes your habit and your culture.

31:04

Ashok K. Pandey: I will give you one example. I was at Mayo College. Five or six of us joined in my cohort, but the rest of the people had been there for ten, fifteen, twenty, thirty years. It was a 132-year-old college when I joined. One day the principal announced in the staff room that there was a conference coming up on continuous and comprehensive evaluation, and asked who would like to go. I thought, I am the junior-most, where do I figure, all these veterans are sitting next to me, somebody else will get the opportunity. And believe me, for two minutes nobody raised a hand. Suddenly I realised — and I raised both my hands, to draw the principal’s attention. He had been getting embarrassed for those two minutes, and he immediately lapped it up: “Oh yes, Ashok, I knew you would do it.” And I went for a four-day conference. What that did for me, only I know. What the others lost, I also know.

32:07

Ashok K. Pandey: So you have to take the initiative. You have to come forward. You have to come out of your comfort zone. If you want to be proud of yourself, if you want to make a difference in somebody else’s life, you have to work hard at it. That is the second thing.

32:18

Ashok K. Pandey: Read a lot. Let information come from all sides. Network — that is very important. I tell maths teachers: do you know the best maths teacher in any other school? Why can’t you make a collective of ten maths teachers from ten schools and meet once a month, at least online, and learn from each other? And find a mentor within the school, and also outside the school. Only then will you know what mentoring is capable of. Those are the four or five things a young teacher should do.

32:57

Ashok K. Pandey: And don’t get bogged down by bad experiences. I was talking to the CEO of an education company who has come up the hard way — teacher, principal, director, now CEO. I asked about her experience and she said: I wanted to quit ten times in my first six months. But her mentor said, do everything but never quit. You have got this position not because you have been ordinary; you have got it because you are extraordinary. And now your test is to be extra patient. She said she overcame it, and today she has done seven years in that position.

The Chanakya principle — spotting hidden potential

33:44

Kavleen Kaur: You have shared such practical tips, and we have talked about the benefits that accrue. I hope teachers are motivated by the passion with which you talk about building the next generation of citizens and leaders.

34:04

Ashok K. Pandey: Since you talked about motivation, two things. Teachers must know their potential. All teachers are like Hanuman — they have huge potential, but they don’t know it. So I am prepared to be a Jambavan for them and remind them of the immense potential they have, and that they can change the life of generations. And I want to share with them the example of Chanakya, who saw an ordinary child in a nondescript place and picked him up, saw the potential in him, taught him communication, statecraft, negotiation, administration — and he became the founder of a dynasty that lasted 140 years. The Maurya dynasty.

U40, the National Mission for Mentoring & self-development resources

34:49

Ashok K. Pandey: That is the potential — and that is the idea behind U40, which GEMS Education has come out with. We cannot answer the problem of mentoring on our own. We can only create models with intention. This is a small model but a great intention, and if it can be replicated, we can make a huge difference. That is exactly what the Government of India also wants to do. Mentoring has been identified in the National Education Policy, and that is where the national mentoring mission comes in. Teachers should read about it, talk about it, and reap the benefits of it.

35:56

Kavleen Kaur: Since you’ve talked about U40, are there resources teachers can pick up themselves — taking ownership of their own growth towards leadership roles?

36:05

Ashok K. Pandey: First of all this opportunity should be management-driven and leadership-driven. All managements and leaders must intentionally embed mentoring and developmental opportunities for all teachers. But at the same time there is something called a self-driven opportunity, and that is what you are hinting at. Nowadays online courses are available — Coursera, Udemy, edX and several others. But the Indian system has also developed: the SWAYAM platform, the NISHTHA platform, the DIKSHA platform. Some of the IITs, like Bombay and Madras, have developed new courses. There is no dearth of opportunity where you can just sit with your computer and learn something new.

36:52

Ashok K. Pandey: Teachers can go to the national mentoring mission portal on the National Council for Teacher Education site and enrol themselves as a mentee. If not every day, then at least twice or thrice a week, a mentoring programme by some expert takes place. Anything that interests you, anything aligned with your domain — you can attend that programme. There is no dearth of opportunities. You can even search and create a plan with the help of AI for your own development and mentoring.

37:25

Kavleen Kaur: So AI could be your mentor as well.

37:30

Ashok K. Pandey: AI could be your mentor also. AI could be your colleague. You can share some of your dilemmas and tensions. You can use it beautifully. You can share your career aspirations and create a plan together to achieve them.

Rapid fire round

37:52

Kavleen Kaur: A non-education interest that quietly made you better at your job in education?

38:10

Ashok K. Pandey: I was not a great sportsman before I joined. I did play volleyball — in fact I played at college level — but it was not integral to me. When I became a teacher, one of my students taught me how to play squash. After that I became a squash coach. I played for veterans, and that changed my whole attitude about teaching and learning. When I said in one of the segments that children have great potential and you can learn from them — I became a squash player because of one of my students. There is co-learning at play.

38:52

Kavleen Kaur: A moment that may have pulled you away from being an educator — and what kept you in the profession?

39:03

Ashok K. Pandey: I came to this profession because my university professor encouraged me. Even then I thought I would give it two or three years, have some fun, get a salary, enjoy life, and then go back to whatever I wanted to do. But in those two or three years I realised there was no other career I could fit into. Being honest to your question — at many points I thought I should change profession. And then I remembered my students, my colleagues, the fun I had. I could go to the library and read a hundred books. I could play for two, three, four hours. Every year I went on tour with the children. And I thought that if I went into any other profession, I would miss all of this.

39:52

Kavleen Kaur: A belief about school leadership that has evolved for you over the years?

39:58

Ashok K. Pandey: All you do is because of your teachers. All you do is because of your students and parents. But you are a fortunate person as a leader, because you get the credit for everything. Therefore my message to my fellow colleagues is: be grateful for the opportunity you have been given. And pay that gratitude every day, in terms of investing in the development of the teachers. They will take that investment back to the children, the parents will be happy and satisfied, and then they come back to you and become an ally of the school.

40:46

Kavleen Kaur: One capability India needs to invest in for developing educational leaders?

40:55

Ashok K. Pandey: Love for India. That is the shortest answer, but let me explain a little. We are already aiming at 2047, to become a developed nation. When we wanted independence, everybody participated — young children, adolescents, women, the differently abled. Now, a hundred years on, we don’t have to fight for independence. We have to fight so that nobody is poor, nobody is hungry, our standard of living rises, we remain a united country in harmony and peace and love with each other — and of course our indices of growth compare with the best in the world. That is what a developed nation is about.

41:43

Ashok K. Pandey: What are the instruments of that? It is more important for school leaders and teachers. We have to tell our children that we have a civilisational history; we have to go back to our roots. There is a lot we have to learn and a lot we have to be grateful about. Second, we are a diverse country — we have to embrace diversity, respect diversity, celebrate diversity. Third, we have been a victim of colonisation for many years, and we have to reclaim and decolonise ourselves in many ways. And fourth, we have to work for character. If we can do these four things in the name of our nation, we will create an example for the world too. I don’t want India to be isolated. India has always been a friend of the world — in fact a teacher to the world — but we have to reclaim it.

42:40

Kavleen Kaur: Your mentors and teachers shaped you. Anyone you would like to mention?

42:56

Ashok K. Pandey: Many. My first thought goes to my middle-school headmaster. It would take another two hours to describe him, but I will tell you one thing: I have not forgotten the image of him mounting an elephant to go to the railway station, to catch a train to Delhi, to receive the President’s Award from Dr. S. Radhakrishnan. He was the first. Then my first principal, under whose wings I became a good human being and a good teacher, Colonel Prem Saran Satsangi, who is no more. And there are many others. I would not like to forget my mentor Dr. Shayama Chona — the legendary doyenne of public school education — who helped me grow at various stages of my life.

44:00

Ashok K. Pandey: One day she said to me — I am not disclosing the place, because it might affect some people — “What are you doing here? I think you are stagnating. You have to do this.” That is the role of a principal, the role of a mentor: with some sense of moral authority and some sense of love and affection, you can say what is right for the person. I have been very fortunate in that sense.

Final message: preserving humanity in leadership

44:26

Ashok K. Pandey: Since we began with AI, I will make my final request to school leaders — because I know they are the influencers for the teachers and the students. You will not be known by how much technology you have implemented, or how good your school is at AI. The future will judge you by whether your leadership was able to preserve humanity.

44:50

Ashok K. Pandey: Because when children come back — and I tell you from my experience — they never say “this teacher taught me this and I became good.” All of them only talk about emotional connections. This teacher valued me. This teacher made me feel happy. This teacher, when I was lonely, when I thought I had lost everything, came and offered her shoulder and I leaned on it. That is what they remember, and that is the role of the teacher. If we do not reclaim and hold on to that role, we will be in the danger zone.

45:27

Ashok K. Pandey: So far, the World Economic Forum’s Future of Jobs statistics say that among the jobs which will disappear, teaching, counselling, advising and mentoring sit very safe — there is only about a thirteen to fourteen per cent chance of replacement, whereas there are other jobs with an eighty to ninety per cent chance. Let us be happy about that — but let us not take it for granted either. That is the role of leadership.

46:00

Kavleen Kaur: That is a very important message. Sincere thanks for sharing your experience, your knowledge and your guidance.

46:08

Ashok K. Pandey: Thank you very much for having me on your show. I have enjoyed every moment of our conversation.
